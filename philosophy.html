Language Teaching Philosophy

My beliefs about how a language is learned, what a beginning Chinese classroom should feel like, and the principles that guide my instruction.

00 In three words

Communicative.
Cultural. Human.

Everything below unfolds from three commitments. They are simple to state and, I am learning, endlessly demanding to practice.

交 交
Principle 01

Communicative

Language is something we do together. Tasks with real outcomes, more student talk than teacher talk, and errors treated as evidence of trying.

文 文
Principle 02

Culturally meaningful

Language use reflects cultural practices and perspectives. Students explore these connections through meaningful examples and intercultural exchange.

人 人
Principle 03

Learner-centered

Safety to speak, clear goals, frequent low-stakes practice, and feedback that names what went well before what comes next.

01 Starting point

I came to language teaching from literature. For years my work has been about reading closely — noticing how a single color in Eileen Chang, a shift of tone in a Yangzhou storyteller, or the layout of a Hong Kong street carries meaning that a dictionary cannot hold. Teaching Chinese to beginners has taught me the reverse lesson: that meaning also begins in the smallest acts of speech. A student who manages to ask a classmate's name in Chinese for the first time, and receives an answer, has crossed a border. My job is to make that crossing happen as often, and as joyfully, as possible.

Three principles guide how I plan and run my CHI 101 classroom: language should be communicative, culturally meaningful, and learner-centered.

Communicative: language is something we do together

I believe students learn a language by using it for real purposes with real people. Grammar and vocabulary matter, but they are tools for communication rather than the goal of it. I aim to introduce new structures in context, give students practice in pairs and small groups, and put those structures to use in tasks that have an outcome — introducing a partner, finding out who in the room has a sibling, agreeing on a time to meet. I aim to keep explanations concise and make space for student talk. I treat mistakes as part of learning and seek to offer corrections that help students continue communicating.

Because Chinese is a tonal language with a writing system unlike anything most of my students have seen, I also believe beginners need generous, structured input before they are asked to produce: hearing tones modeled again and again, seeing characters in meaningful phrases before writing them alone, and using pinyin to support pronunciation while gradually building character recognition.

A beginner's first successful sentence in Chinese is not a small thing. It is the moment the language stops being a subject and becomes a voice.

Culturally meaningful: language carries a world

As a scholar of Chinese literature and dialects, I cannot separate language from the culture that shaped it — and I do not want to. Even at the elementary level, the way Chinese names are ordered, how people address a teacher or an elder, what one says on receiving a gift, or why a greeting might ask whether you have eaten, opens a window into ways of living and thinking. I bring these into the classroom not as trivia but as part of what it means to speak the language well. Culture, for me, also means the living variety of Chinese: I want my students to know that the Mandarin they are learning coexists with Cantonese, Shanghainese, and the Yangzhou dialect I have studied, and that this diversity is a richness, not a complication.

At the same time, cultural learning runs in both directions. My students come from many linguistic and cultural backgrounds, and their questions and comparisons make the classroom a place of genuine intercultural exchange. I try to design activities where their own lives — families, hometowns, daily routines — become the content we talk about in Chinese.

Learner-centered: confidence before perfection

Learning a new language as an adult takes courage. I believe my most important responsibility is to build an environment in which students feel safe enough to speak, and supported enough to keep going when it is hard. Concretely, this means clear objectives for every lesson so students know what they are working toward; frequent low-stakes practice so that no single moment carries too much weight; varied activities that reach different kinds of learners; and honest, specific feedback that names what a student did well before pointing to the next step.

It also means paying attention to individual students. In two sections of CHI 101 I meet learners with very different reasons for being there — heritage connections, career plans, curiosity, love of film or music. I try to learn these reasons early and to let them shape examples, tasks, and the encouragement each student needs.

Assessment as part of learning

I see assessment as information for both of us, not a verdict. Quizzes, oral checks, and short performance tasks tell me what to reteach and tell students where they stand. I aim for assessments that resemble real language use — a short dialogue, a self-introduction, a message to a friend — so that preparing for them is itself good practice. My goal is to share criteria in advance and offer timely feedback that students can use in their next attempt.

Growing as a teacher

This is my first year teaching at a university in the United States, and I approach it as a learner. I plan to learn through classroom observation, student feedback, reflection, and reading in second language acquisition and Chinese pedagogy, connecting classroom experience with research. The Chinese phrase 教學相長 — teaching and learning strengthen one another — is the principle I keep returning to. My students teach me how to teach them; I hope, in turn, to give them a language they can carry into the world.
